The problem

Deciding to buy took one tap. Buying took three pages.

Most purchases went through full-page checkout. Tapping Proceed to buy takes the customer away from the page they decided on. First comes Select a Payment Method, a full page listing every way to pay. Then Place Your Order, another full page with the order summary, payment, address and delivery date. Only then is there a Pay now button.

Customers want to review everything on one screen and pay. Every extra page is a chance to drop off, and in emerging markets far more customers dropped off on these pages than in established ones. Meanwhile, quick-commerce apps had taught customers to check out right from the cart.

The decision happens on the page. Checkout should too.

An in-context checkout already existed. It wasn’t enough.

In some markets, Buy Now could already open a checkout sheet. It was fast, but research showed where it fell short, and why customers either gave up or fell back to the longer full-page checkout:

  1. Cards only. No Pay on Delivery, UPI or pay later, the ways many customers in emerging markets prefer to pay.
  2. Hidden edits. Customers didn’t realise the “Deliver to” and “Pay with” headers could be tapped to change them.
  3. Lost in panels. Changes opened a second panel. Customers didn’t see that their order had updated, closed the sheet by accident, or pressed back on Android and lost it all.
  4. No item checks. No quantity, size or Prime delivery to confirm it was the right product.
  5. One item at a time. No way to buy a phone and its case together, or to reach free delivery.

The idea

Checkout from anywhere. A bigger basket on the way.

The mental model: enable frictionless checkout from any touchpoint on Amazon, while surfacing contextual basket-building opportunities that drive higher order value.

Principles

  1. Fast, and transparent

    Speed only works when nothing is a surprise. The total, delivery charges, savings and any cheaper or faster option are shown before paying.

  2. Change it without leaving

    Delivery, address and payment come filled in from the customer’s defaults or last choices. Tap anywhere on a card to change it, right there in the sheet.

  3. Built for the everyday purchase

    Optimised for the simple purchases most people make. Complex ones, like scheduled installation, still go to full-page checkout.

Decision making

The old sheet hid every change behind a header link and a second panel. Two questions shaped the redesign: how to make editing obvious, and how much to show before the customer asks.

Cards answered the first: each is one clear, tappable block, so there’s nothing to discover. For the second we explored two ways: reveal other choices only when a card is tapped, or show them upfront as buttons whenever a cheaper or faster option exists.

Where it hands off

The sheet is built for speed, so it doesn’t try to do everything. Adding a new address or card, gift options and scheduled deliveries still go to full-page checkout, where there’s room to do them properly.
